The ClearFrame guide how it works, and how to direct it

ClearFrame turns research reports, podcasts, scripts and briefs into narrated motion-graphics films, drawn natively in Rust. This guide covers the pipeline, the vocabulary and, above all, how to ask for a film worth watching.

  • Last updated: 2026-10-14
  • Covers 0.5.0 (unreleased)
  • 31 blocks + stages · 15 palettes
  • Open creative library · editable storyboards

Technical films and review

Start a PR explainer with new FILM --playbook pr-walkthrough --treatment business. A system is one canvas.props.diagram: components with stable ids (user, service, database, queue, state, external), connectors (dashed, flowing) and groups, changed over time by steps — add, remove, replace … with, set a status, and send a request along the connections — each cued to a spoken word. Beats that share node ids morph, so the system carries across cuts instead of being redrawn. clearframe sketch architecture (or component-change, state-machine) prints an editable starting diagram. Layout follows the connections and adapts to wide and tall frames; connectors take separate ports and runs and detour around components. Diagram contract.

How to ask: “Explain PR 214 from the diff: show the old request path, mark the removed call, add the queue in its place, and send one request through the new path.”

Business frames stay steady: the treatment sets the film camera: "none" (a default for every beat, including ones added later; plates then hold still) and lens.blur: 0; diagram beats hold still by default. check warns when a plate's drift stacks on footage that already moves. Motion explains a change; a reading hold is valid.

Choose background or half-screen B-roll before generation (a diagram can take area on one half). plan lists footage that cannot cover its beat plus offset, beats longer than one generated take, and the same seconds shown twice; clips refuses to pay for such a take, and looping is rejected. Inspect text against moving footage at opening, middle, end and lighting changes, including phone size. Blur alone is insufficient. B-roll review.

Watch, leave notes, let the agent edit. viewer --serve is a page for the people a film is for, not for editors. The home page asks What are we making? (your words, wide or tall, any files) and creates the project with its own OpenCode conversation, which drafts a first cut. A film opens on its picture and a filmstrip of its scenes. Click anywhere on the picture to leave a sticky note on that spot at that moment (it keeps the words on screen under it), or write one for the whole film; Send N notes to the agent sends them as one message. The Agent tab beside the picture holds the conversation: one folded line of steps per request, the reply, approvals for commands and paid sound, Stop, and Undo for the newest request's edits. The agent answers each note it acted on in one line; you say Looks good or Not yet, and approve a version in your own words. New versions appear while the page is open. Opened as a file, the page plays films and keeps notes in the browser, and the notes go out as a message to paste into Claude Code. The studio page · setup in agent-studio.md.

How to ask: pin a note on the chart's label, “this should land on the word ‘half’”, then a second on the closing card, “end on the question, not the logo”, and send both.

01the short version

What it is

ClearFrame is an agent-directed motion-graphics system. A model (usually Claude) reads your material and writes one storyboard.json. The scene engine then draws every frame on the GPU (Skia on Metal) from a compiled scene plan: native stages — actors that keep their identity, packets on links, code edits read from a commit, footage, materials, particles, a moving camera with real motion blur — and the blocks' display type, counting numbers, honest charts, diagrams, hand-drawn canvases and captions, each timed to the words of the narration. fframes runs the render around it: it encodes GOP-aligned segments of the film in parallel and joins them. Google models supply voice, music, stills and the occasional footage insert, but only when you ask for them and set a budget. It has no browser engine and no timeline editor. You direct in words, and the storyboard is the whole film.

Research → explainer

Reports become arguments

ingest --markdown pulls every figure out with its sentence and source, along with the tensions, quotes and chart-ready tables. It also scaffolds a question-led research-digest film. The sweet spot is 60–90 s, which is 12–18 beats.

Podcast → clips

Recordings become social cuts

ingest --audio slices a recording into gapless beats with measured word timings and speaker tags. Vertical clips default to pop captions, so the words stay on screen even when a beat turns into a picture.

Script → film

Narration gets pictures

Bring finished words. Each beat gets one idea, one landing word and its own picture. Recording the whole narration as one continuous take keeps one consistent voice performing from line to line.

It also handles workplace and client films (benefits, policy, training, market updates), product reveals, data stories, lessons, pull request explainers, one-world journeys through a process or place, and speech-led kinetic type. Run playbooks for the current starting arcs, including financial mechanisms and scenario comparisons.

What it is not

  • Not a slide-deck maker. If the result looks like slides, the film has failed. critique and the review loop exist to catch that.
  • Not a text-to-video generator. Facts, charts and labels are drawn natively. Generated footage is a sparing insert (the default review threshold is 20% of runtime) with its own audio discarded, and it never carries information.
  • Not a fact-checker. It refuses numbers that have no visible source, but it can't tell whether the source is right. The facts are your responsibility.
  • Not a GUI or a browser renderer. The old HTML/GSAP engine is archived for recovery only. The scene engine is the only renderer; fframes, which once drew the blocks, is back only as the runtime that schedules, encodes and joins the frames the scene engine draws.

02from material to MP4

How it works

Material flows in one direction, from the source to storyboard.json to the frames. Direction flows back through a review loop. You change the film by editing the storyboard, and you change the tool by fixing the engine.

  1. MaterialReports (Markdown, text, HTML, DOCX, RTF, or PDF via pdftotext), recordings with word timestamps, a finished script or a brief. A reference film is optional: reference VIDEO writes REFERENCE.md with its cut rhythm, a keyframe sheet, its palette (with the nearest theme) and its motion energy.
  2. Ingest & directioningest --markdown writes BRIEF.md (figures with their sentences and sources, tensions, questions, quotes, tables) and a storyboard for you to rewrite. ingest --audio imports a recording as beats. new DIR --playbook NAME --treatment ID scaffolds a film from a starting arc and writes DIRECTION.md. The story spine goes at the top of DIRECTION.md: the question, the misconception, the turn and the payoff.
  3. storyboard.jsonThis is the single source. It holds the film settings (palette, motion, text motion, captions, sound, voice) and an ordered list of beats. Each beat is a block with validated props, a vo line and optional layers. It is checked against schema/storyboard.schema.json and per-block validators.
  4. Voice & timingvoice --draft records a free local voice. Without --draft, it calls Gemini TTS, which is paid. align --whisper measures word timings locally for free, align --words imports a word file, and align --transcribe is paid. A beat lasts lead + voice + tail, which defaults to 0.25 s + voice + 0.6 s. Measured timings are tied to the hash of the audio file.
  5. job.json + plan.jsonThe engine turns every spoken cue into a time, schedules staged items so they finish inside their beat, compiles native stages into the scene plan, prepares the media under content-hash names and writes build/native/job.json, build/native/plan.json and build/timing.json.
  6. Scene engineThe Rust engine draws each frame on the GPU as a pure function of the frame number and the prepared inputs: the film's backdrop, texture, lens and chrome, each beat's block (built as a display list of shapes, text and pictures and painted on the GPU), native stages between and over them, and dissolves as two real layers. Footage is decoded by FFmpeg at the size it is drawn. There is no random state and no wall-clock animation, so any frame can be re-rendered exactly, and stills, sheets, previews and the final all call the same frame. Scene engine.
  7. MP4render --draft (or preview) uses a fast encoder, about 3× faster than the final. The final render verifies dimensions, FPS and decoded frames, then writes build/video.mp4 with a provenance file recording input hashes, renderer revision, encoder, audio provenance, output hash and colour space.

The review loop

For full 1080p finals, allow at least five minutes in the calling harness for rendering and audio finishing, or resume the same running process session. Longer films may need more time. On this 8 GB Mac, run one full render or test suite at a time; see film/SETUP.md for the render command.

critique is instant and needs no render. It flags deck-like runs, stillness, text density, weak hooks and "and then" story chains. check validates inputs and native diagnostics, and runs a frame audit that fails on held type cut by the frame edge, type over type or over a 3D subject, and type too small to read. sheet renders a contact sheet at three moments per beat. The draft MP4 is the thing to watch and listen to, and review DIR decodes frames around every cut and word boundary. qa DIR reads the whole encoded film for the bugs a still cannot show: a one-frame pop, a stretch where the picture barely changes under the voice (it measures change per second against the frame a second earlier), a world cut that jumps, an untagged export, loudness off target, a drop heard in the wrong place. It writes a one-frame-per-second timeline.png and a 360 px phone.png. Last comes a fresh reviewer: a separate subagent that did not write the film and judges it against DIRECTION.md.

node engine/cli.mjs new my-film --playbook data-story --treatment editorial
node engine/cli.mjs critique my-film          # instant: rhythm, density, hook, story links
node engine/cli.mjs draft my-film             # critique + draft voice + check + sheet + draft MP4
node engine/cli.mjs align my-film --whisper   # free, local, measured word timings
node engine/cli.mjs render my-film            # final encode

Review & edit: rough cut, notes, candidates

The person's first look is not the finished film. It is a plan they can read (paper DIR: chapters, timecodes, who speaks, the picture each beat is meant to get, the transcript) and then a rough cut: the whole film in motion on the real renderer, made with existing blocks and captions, with pictures that aren't drawn yet declared as placeholder slates (draft DIR --rough). Bespoke drawing and reviewer rounds come after their notes. For a recording, the words stay as recorded unless they ask for cuts.

Every full render saves a revision in review/ with its inputs kept by content, and review/index.html plays it beside the transcript, with a note box that stamps the revision and playhead. A note such as "at 2:13 the diagram is confusing, keep the voice" is anchored to that cut: the beat, the words around the playhead, their time in the recording. After later edits it is found again by content, never by timestamp: current, moved, changed (maybe already fixed), stale, orphaned (its passage was cut) or addressed. An edit becomes a candidate (revise) with a before/after preview of only the stretch it touched, rendered from the full timeline with the film's own mix, and a plain report of what changed in content, what only looks different because of a neighbour or a film setting, and what merely moved. The person accepts, refines or rejects; applied is not accepted, and an agent's one-shot decision is never shown as acceptance.

--rough relaxes only what is declared unfinished (placeholder slates and elements marked unfinished): a clipped source line, label or caption still fails, as do facts, audio and timing checks. "Keep the voice" keeps the recording and allows only the cuts the person asks for. Recorded narration is cut, split and merged from the untouched source recording, with measured timing intact and byte-identical undo. The contract, with every state and limit, is docs/editing.md.

node engine/cli.mjs paper my-podcast                       # the plan to read
node engine/cli.mjs draft my-podcast --rough               # full-length rough cut → r001 + review/index.html
node engine/cli.mjs note my-podcast "the diagram is confusing" --at 2:13 --rev r001 --keep voice --by Ana
node engine/cli.mjs revise my-podcast --note n001          # candidate + before/after passages
node engine/cli.mjs accept my-podcast r002 --note n001 --by Ana --said "yes, clearer"
node engine/cli.mjs cut my-podcast --words "a story for another day" --by Ana   # undo: uncut --cut c001
node engine/cli.mjs runlog my-podcast                      # measured time per command; gaps are not work

03the nouns you'll direct with

Core concepts

Beatsthe unit of a film

A beat is one idea. It has an id, a block, props and usually a vo line. It can also carry style (voice energy), chapter, transition/exit, tone, camera, plate, art, textMotion, heading and timing (lead, tail, hold, duration). Aim for 5–15 spoken words per beat. critique warns above 40.

Blocks & props31 native blocks

The blocks fall into families: story, numbers, charts, decisions, diagrams, text, media, speech, graphics and motion (see the library tables). blocks NAME prints the props and a ready-to-paste example. Headline blocks take emphasis (up to four whole-word phrases in the accent colour), emphasisStyle: "serif" for the italic feeling word, and align: "center".

Establish the picture before the late word. A staged item’s say overrides the block’s land. The pacing check now reads those item times. For beats longer than 8–10 seconds, plan the lead-in and the hold: opening scenery, a slow push, restrained particles, or reviewed stillness. Use at: 0 and enter: "none" for background art that must be present immediately.

Spoken cuesland · say

land, growSay, drawSay, per-item say and bar focus.say take an exact word or phrase from the vo line, or local seconds. So do the canvas fields say, exitSay, keys[].say and along.say. Digits and number words match either way ("18" finds "eighteen"). Reveals start just early so they settle on the word, and counts pre-roll so the figure finishes as it is said. A missing cue fails, and so does a cue too late to finish its entrance.

Canvas & artdraw what blocks can't

Declare fills on outlines. Stroked circles, ellipses and rectangles still default to a solid accent fill. Set fill: "none" for contour rings and wireframes, or an explicit colour for a filled shape. The checks warn about omissions in canvas and nested art.

canvas is a beat you draw. It offers 16 element types with entrances, keyframes, motion along paths, loops and exits, all cueable to speech. Three of them point at real content instead of at coordinates: marks on a text element ring, underline, box, strike, cross or highlight its own words, or stamp a short verdict over them; a document is a page of real text that scrolls to the section the voice reads; a pointer presses elements by id, the step in the app (FSA deadline uses all three). Any beat can also carry art.under / art.over layers made of the same elements, placed in frame pixels: an arrow onto a bar, a circle round a number. Start from a sketch and place things with still --grid. view: "auto" fits a drawing into the space below the title.

To show how something behaves, four props draw a picture that changes, from a formula or a labelled simulation: graph, accumulate, estimate and field. They show behaviour, never data (canvas, the clearframe-explain skill).

Native stagesidentities that move

A stage is drawn by the engine itself on the GPU. Say what happens and the recipe places it: actors (a card per component, its id is its identity), links that follow them, events — send a packet along a link, state, pulse, move, callout, burst, camera — each cued to a spoken word. code: {commit, file, window} shows an edit read from git with lines that keep their place. Elements use the canvas vocabulary plus video, shader, material, connector, attach and large particles; shutter adds real motion blur. Use a stage block for the whole picture, a beat stage under or over any block, or film stages that keep the same actors across several beats. Stages.

Worldsone drawing, a moving camera

Consecutive canvas beats that share props.world form one continuous drawing. Each beat draws only what is new, and everything drawn earlier stays (still looping). The cuts between them are invisible, and the camera travels between camera rects view: [x, y, w, h]. End on a wide view of the whole world. A world survives an interruption: a world beat after a kinetic card resumes where the camera left off. world DIR renders the map with every beat's camera rect numbered.

{ "id": "source", "block": "canvas", "props": { "world": "water", "view": [0, 0, 1920, 1080], "elements": [ …mountains, rain… ] } },
{ "id": "plant",  "block": "canvas", "props": { "world": "water", "view": [1500, 0, 1920, 1080], "elements": [ …the pipe, the plant… ] } },
{ "id": "whole",  "block": "canvas", "props": { "world": "water", "view": [-100, -560, 5200, 2925], "elements": [ …one line of type… ] } }

Treatmentsart direction in one word

A treatment sets the palette, type voice (film.type: the display family and emphasis for titles, statements, endcards, chapters and kinetic text; types lists inter, geometric and bookish), backdrop, texture, frame, motion, transition grammar, voice style and sound, plus some beat defaults. It also writes its rules into DIRECTION.md. Each is a file in library/treatments/, and a project can add its own. The ten built-ins are workplace, editorial, business, explanation, sketchbook, blueprint, audiogram, research-study, keynote and cutpaper. Apply one with new DIR --playbook NAME --treatment ID, then adapt it. It is a starting direction, not a limit.

Palettescontrast-checked on load

Every palette has the tokens bg, surface, ink, muted, accent, accent2, positive and negative. You can override any of them with hex, for example "theme": {"base": "ink", "accent": "#d6acff"}, and check warns if the override drops contrast. Canvas colours are tokens too, so art follows the palette and a beat's tone. looks DIR --beat ID renders one frame in every palette.

Motion & text motionhow things arrive

motion.preset is gentle, snappy or spring, and intensity (0–1) scales travel. Values never overshoot, and a pop settles in from 86% of its size without passing full size under any preset. textMotion controls how display type arrives: lines (the default), words, letters or cascade. Use one mode for the film and break it for one or two moments. Scene headings always rise as a line. heading: "bottom" sets a scene's title as a lower third.

Transitionscuts with meaning

The entrances are cut, fade, rise, wipe, push and zoom, plus one graphic one, panel, that carries one movement across the cut. Exits mirror the next entrance automatically, and scenes never cross-dissolve. A panel belongs at a turn in the story. Use cut for rhythm and push for sequence. The retired iris, whip, flash and dissolve are refused by name, with what to use instead.

Captionsstandard · pop · kinetic

Every narrated film is captioned unless it sets captions: false, to a broadcast standard (after Netflix's English timed-text guide): at most two lines of 42 characters (28 on a vertical frame), timed to the measured words, white Inter SemiBold on a translucent near-black box that hugs each line, cut on and off with the speech and never across a cut. A caption whose picture has type in the bottom band moves to the top, and check warns when a caption still covers the picture's type. The same cues go to the SRT and VTT. "pop" is the social style for vertical clips: a few words at a time in heavy type, the spoken word in an accent pill. The kinetic block makes speech itself the picture, in highlight, reveal, word or stack mode. Final captioned or kinetic renders need measured word timings (speech).

Sources & honestynon-negotiable

Every displayed number needs a visible props.source and an entry in storyboard.sources, or check refuses it. That covers charts, stats and canvas counts. Record the claim, the source and the as-of date. Illustrative figures must be labelled as such on screen. Sample figures and fictional quotes are never evidence. Generated imagery is illustration, never documentation. Never imitate real people, logos or documents, and disclose synthetic voices.

04what actually moved quality

Getting great outcomes

These are the principles that turned narrated slides into designed films. Each comes with the prop or command that puts it into practice.

  1. Films, not slides.The tells are every beat sharing a layout, a heading over a static graphic, the same fade on every cut, nothing moving once it has entered, and no imagery. Vary the frame: a full-bleed plate, a tone colour block, a kinetic stack, a centred statement, a lower-third heading, or no heading at all. critique counts heading-over-graphic scenes and flags three scenes of the same family in a row.
  2. Story before pictures.Question → misconception → turn → payoff. Choose 3–5 claims and send everything else to the source card. Link beats with but or therefore, never "and then". Read the vo lines alone: they should make an argument you could follow with your eyes closed.
  3. One picture per idea.Choose the picture by what the sentence does, not by what it is about (docs/ideas.md). When the narration says how, why, through, between, until or instead, draw the mechanism rather than the words.
  4. Land on the word.Each beat has one landing word (two at most), the stressed word at the end of the sentence. Cue the visual to it with land or say. Counts finish as the number is said, and world cameras arrive by the new line's first word.
  5. Travel instead of cutting.A process, a place, a chain of causes or a system should be one world with a travelling camera, not five separate drawings. Lay stations out in story order so the camera never re-crosses old material, and finish with a pull-back to the opening shot, changed.
  6. Punctuate rarely.Use one or two tone scenes per minute and a panel only at a turn. Everything else cuts, pushes or fades. critique warns when punctuation stops meaning anything.
  7. The picture changes with the voice.Something changes on each stressed word: a coin drops, a lock opens, a clause is marked, a value is pressed. Hold still on purpose (a reading hold, pace: "hold"), and never add wobble or ambient loops to fill a hold. draft's motion verdict measures how long the picture sits still under the voice.
  8. Look, then ask a fresh reviewer.Diagnostics can't judge taste. Open the sheet, watch the draft, then have a reviewer with no authorship bias judge it against DIRECTION.md. Keep the same reviewer across rounds so it can say which notes are resolved. Sort its notes into tool problems (fix the engine and every film benefits) and film choices (fix the storyboard).
Per minute of film, aim for
  • at least one drawn mechanism (canvas), one colour-block punctuation, and a panel or push at the turn;
  • imagery (plates) where the world matters, and kinetic type where the words are the picture;
  • a hook that lands inside 3 s, about 3 on-screen words per second, and narration at 140–160 wpm.

Films, not slides: the film score

These films are sent by companies to employees and clients. They stop being slides because the picture changes with the voice, not because they borrow a film look: there are no letterbox bars, grades, flash cuts or swells in the library. critique prints a film score (film N/100) from the eight tells of a slideshow, and each tell it finds names the fix. The tells, the shot list to write before the blocks (each beat's scale, its camera and why, and the cut into the next shot), a subject that stays on screen and a camera that moves for a reason are in skills/clearframe-motion/SKILL.md.

Film choices that moved it

  • Stations laid out in story order: the sky up, the roof in close, the street plan down, the survey beside it.
  • The world changes state (a day sky, a roof filling with heat) instead of cutting to a new picture.
  • One interruption, at the turn, and the world resumes afterwards.
  • Labels leave at an exitAt after their beat, tidying the world while the camera is away.
  • A callback to the opening number at the end.

Tool problems fixed in the engine (0.5.0)

  • Camera moves landed late. They now pre-roll into the outgoing beat's settled tail and arrive by the new line's first word.
  • dur: 0 keys caused single-frame flashes. Zero-length keys now apply on their own frame.
  • A world lost its place after a card. It now resumes from the camera's last view.
  • Word-by-word headings read as speech-synced. Headings now always rise as a line.
  • Holds froze once the camera arrived. viewDrift now keeps a world alive.

The model does the authoring. Your job is to hand over material, intent and constraints, and then to direct the review rounds. The fewer creative decisions you leave to guesswork, the better the film.

Voice with Gemini 3.8 TTS

The rule: one continuous take, one short style. Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS keeps one consistent, lively voice when it reads the whole narration in a single request, as one text with one style. Voices drift when lines are recorded separately, each with its own style or inflection, and stitched together: every style re-prompts the voice and every join resets its pitch and energy. ClearFrame therefore records the whole film as one take by default (voice.takes: "film") and cuts it back into beats with measured word timings. Per-beat style is ignored in a continuous take.

08when something's off

Troubleshooting & FAQ

"Heavy-job lock is held by …; the build starts when it finishes."

Native compilation uses one Cargo job. On this small machine, run one full render or test suite at a time; focused checks and editing can proceed. Builds are skipped when the renderer source hasn't changed, and the warm cache makes the rest quick. When parallel agents are working, avoid starting concurrent heavy builds.

"Changes outside note n00N's scope"

revise only accepts edits inside the beats the note is about (found again by content in the working copy). Revert the stray change, or widen the scope with --scope ID,ID --reason "…" and tell the person why. Beats that only moved, or that look different because a neighbour changed, are allowed and listed.

A note is "stale" or "orphaned"

Its moment can't be found honestly in the current cut: the quoted words changed, appear twice, or were cut. It is never moved to whatever now plays at that time. Show the person the old moment (its revision, time and words, or page DIR --rev rNNN) and ask whether it still applies.

"cut --note" refuses: the note is orphaned, stale or already addressed

cut --note and cut --at … --rev cut the exact words that were pointed at, found by their identity in the recording's transcript, never by searching for the same text. If those words are gone (cut earlier, or the note's passage was removed), only partly there, or already cut for that note, nothing is cut. Show the person the old moment and ask; to cut other words, name them with --words and --beat.

Final stays open after the person accepted a fix

Final closes only on a decision about the whole current cut and its final encode: accept DIR rNNN --checkpoint final --by NAME --said "…" after rendering the final. Accepting one note's result or some beats, or an earlier cut, does not approve the film; any change to its content (an audio-only one too), a draft rendered over build/video.mp4, or a later rejection reopens it.

restore says the result is "NOT the same" as the revision

A whole restore writes the revision's files, sets aside later library overrides and shadowing media under review/aside/, then compares the working copy with the revision. "Inputs" means the files match but the renderer or fonts changed since, so frames may differ. "NOT the same" lists what still differs (often a shared library from CLEARFRAME_LIBRARY, which lives outside the project); the command exits 1, and restore DIR with the reported restore point undoes it.

The rough cut still fails the frame audit

--rough relaxes only text that is declared unfinished: a placeholder slate, or an element marked unfinished. A clipped source line, a covered label or a too-small caption is still an error, because roughness says nothing about whether text that informs is readable. Fix it, or, if it really is a stand-in, mark that element unfinished (a final render will refuse it until it is finished).

"uses rough, which the canvas block draws"

A stage element uses a canvas-only feature (rough, print, mosaic, morph, solid, meter, a level loop). Move that element into a canvas beat or art, or remove the feature. Nothing is dropped silently.

"Native build needs N GiB free"

The build refuses to start below 10 GiB free with a warm cache, or 25 GiB for a cold build. doctor reports the same thresholds. Free up space rather than deleting the warm cache: a cold rebuild needs far more.

The draft passed, but the final fails on word timing

Drafts accept estimated narration and word timing. Final kinetic or captioned scenes need measured timestamps tied to the current audio's hash. Run align DIR --whisper (free; install with brew install openai-whisper or pip install openai-whisper), import a word file with align --words, or use the paid align --transcribe. Replacing the audio invalidates the alignment. Interpolated words are never labelled measured. build/timing.json shows each beat's wordTiming and alignmentIssue.

"Spoken cue not found" or "cued too late"

Cues must be words exactly as they appear in the vo line. Digits and number words match either way ("18" ↔ "eighteen"). A cue has to leave room for the entrance to finish inside the beat, so cue an earlier word or give the beat more tail.

"Text reaches outside this beat's camera view" / "renders at about N px"

In worlds, check expects the camera to crop the drawing and ignores "cut off by the canvas edge" while the camera is moving. It still warns when a beat's own text falls outside its view, and when text shrinks below about 22 px through the camera's zoom (the warning gives the minimum size in world units). Fix it by widening or moving the view, enlarging the label, or moving the label into the shot. For vertical cuts, set viewTall. Confirm with world DIR and still --grid.

Why won't it render my number?

Every displayed number (stats, charts, canvas count) needs a visible props.source and a sources entry, and check refuses it otherwise. Professional audiences forgive plain design but not a wrong number, and the storyboard plus its sources is the audit trail. Illustrative figures must say so on screen. Also make sure the number spoken, the number shown and the number in sources agree.

"Beat ends before its counters finish"

A final render fails (and a draft warns) when a count or bar hasn't reached its true value by the end of the beat. Raise the beat's tail or hold, or cue the count earlier.

A panel became a fade

The outgoing scene had no room to finish the cover, so check warns and falls back to a fade. Give that beat more tail, or use a cut. A related warning: the last element landing less than 0.45 s before the cut. Cue it earlier or extend the beat.

"… was retired: …"

The storyboard names something the library audit removed because it fell below the bar for a company or financial film: the iris, whip, flash and dissolve transitions (and transitionOrigin), the glow, dots and mosaic backdrops, the scramble, blur and assemble entrances, the scatter exit, the lens's film looks (letterbox, grade, gradeAmount, bloom, aberration, leak, handheld) and canvas kpi. The message names what to use instead (usually fade, push, cut, none or paper; the kpis or stat block for canvas kpi). Nothing is drawn as a silent substitute.

Captions appeared on a film that had none

Every narrated film is captioned unless it sets captions: false. If check warns that a caption covers the picture's type, keep the bottom band clear in close-ups or let the picture's type sit higher; a caption whose picture has type in the bottom band already moves to the top.

My morph doesn't show

A morph pairs the same element id across consecutive canvas beats. Any transition other than cut hides it, and beats with different views make positions jump.

Text overflows, or a character fails

Text that still doesn't fit its box at 14 px fails. Shorten it or split the beat. The bundled fonts cover Latin, Greek, Cyrillic, arrows, maths and currency, and check names any other character (for example CJK) along with its prop.

Check passes, but it still looks like slides

That's expected. Diagnostics can't judge composition or taste. Run critique, open the sheet, and ask for a fresh review round (see iteration prompts).

A paid command asks for a key, or I don't want to spend

Paid generation needs GEMINI_API_KEY. Rendering, draft voice, draft music, word-file imports and Whisper alignment are all free. Run plan first, and use --budget and --only to scope a paid call. The budget check is an estimate, not a provider billing cap.

Render refuses to write my --out file

Overwriting an existing explicit --out needs --force.

How long, how many beats, how much footage?

60–90 s is about 12–18 beats, with beats of 3–5 s for professional pieces and 2–3 s for social cuts. Narration runs at about 150 wpm. Generated footage stays sparing: the default review threshold is 20% of runtime, clip audio is discarded, and both joins get reviewed (docs/continuity.md).

The cinema score had called a film 100 that sat still for half its length. How to ask: “Make a 60-second film from this plan summary that gets new hires to raise their 401(k) to 5%. Not an HR slideshow.”

Explanations: a picture that changes. Four canvas props draw how something behaves, on any subject. graph shows how an input changes an output: a point rides a curve from a stated formula, such as dose and response, practice and skill, or how busy a desk is and how many wait (an M/M/1 queue). accumulate shows small amounts adding up: steps under a rate lift and stack into the total (savings, rain in a reservoir, a backlog). estimate shows noisy evidence settling: simulated reviews or readings arrive, their average steadies inside a band of ±1 standard error, and the dots gather into the spread. With bias, the average settles just as calmly in the wrong place, because more samples shrink chance, not bias. field shows where things flow: arrows and particles on a chosen field, a qualitative picture, not a fitted crowd or market model. Moves land on words (after times a move from its word), and keys take twelve rate functions adapted from Manim (MIT), such as thereAndBack to point and return or overshoot to settle. The explanation treatment and the clearframe-explain skill (studio guide topic explain) carry the method: intuition before symbols, one change at a time, a colour that means one thing, and holds you can see. Its voice, Charon, is a provisional pick from published descriptions; Iapetus and Schedar are alternatives. How to ask: “Explain why a handful of reviews can mislead, as a picture: the average settling as more arrive.” Settling.

A board: each moment, then the whole. place draws a scene's picture as one panel of a world. The camera holds close on each panel, travels between them, then pulls back to show them all side by side, still moving, the way a showreel reveals that its moments belong together. It moves pictures but does not clip them, and on a phone the overview becomes a narrow column. How to ask: “Show the three moments one by one, then pull back to see them together.”

The studio agent can look at its own renders. clearframe_look hands the agent one bounded picture: a still, or a motion sheet of a rough cut (frames over time, in reading order, each with its time), one scene at a time, or at phone size. It can judge hierarchy, motion and legibility, which the engine's checks cannot, before it reports. This needs a model that reads images; a text-only model is told to say so. How to ask: “Make the rough cut, look at it, and fix anything that doesn't read on a phone.”

One lit path through a maze. The maze sketch draws a maze, then lights the one way through on your word and follows it to the exit. It suits onboarding and guidance. How to ask: “Show that we guide new customers through account setup, as one lit path through a maze.”

A bridge across a gap. The bridge sketch builds a bridge between two cliffs on your word, then sends work across it, again and again. It suits an integration, a partnership or new access. How to ask: “Show our team and the data finally connected, as a bridge being built.”

A vault that closes on a word. The vault sketch shows cards of data flying into an open vault. Then the door swings shut, the wheel turns and the bolts slide home, each on your words. It suits privacy, backups and trust. How to ask: “Show that customer data goes somewhere safe that only they can open.”

Faster, by fewer steps. The shortcut sketch shows speed without inventing a number. An old route winds through four waits and a new one goes straight across. Two tokens leave together at the same speed, and the direct one arrives first. How to ask: “Show that checkout is faster now because it skips the waits.” Fewer steps.

From a tangle to a line. The untangle sketch starts with scattered cards joined by crossing curves and pulsing friction points. On your word the cards glide into one row and a clean line carries the work through them. The pieces stay the same; only the order changes. How to ask: “Show how six tools became one flow, as a tangle that straightens out.” One line.

Light in two inks. The dither material paints a stage shape (or the stage's ground) in two palette inks with an ordered dither under a light that drifts across it. Pixel and retro looks get real light and shade, like a handheld game's product shot. How to ask: “Give the product reveal a 1-bit Game Boy look, lit from the side.”

Scraps become one product. The cut-paper sketch is a cutting mat of coloured scraps that fly into place, one after another, as a product card. The card lifts off the mat as its name types in, and every layer keeps its own paper shadow. It suits feedback becoming a product, a launch, or how something came together. How to ask: “Show our customer notes and sketches coming together into the app, in cut paper.”

A search in the dark. The searchlight sketch is a dark storeroom searched by one torch. The light finds three things in turn, each labelled as it is found, then the lamps come on and a thread joins them: what nobody had looked at, a hidden cause, an audit. sketchSay lands a sketch's moments on your words ({"FIRST": "logs", "ALL": "Lights"}), just as sketchText names its placeholders. How to ask: “Show the three things slowing the app at night as a search in a dark room, then turn the lights on.”

Ask the library in plain words. find "a playful teaser for a retro game" searches one index of everything a film can be built from: blocks, sketches, playbooks, directions, looks, palettes and type voices, cast shapes and moves, stage mechanisms and the curated examples. It returns a short mixed shortlist, each entry saying what it is and when it fits; find --id cast-shape:server prints one entry's exact authoring with an example to copy. A project's own library entries take part. The studio agent uses the same index, so asking it for ideas returns a few distinct options with reasons rather than a tour of every catalog. The clearframe-inspiration skill (studio guide topic inspiration) is the method: a brief becomes two or three concepts that differ in the picture's idea, each with a reason in everyday words, then the agent picks one unless you do. How to ask: “Give me three different ways to show our faster checkout, and say who each one suits.” Library index.

Ten new cast objects and a pixel look. A server, a database, a cloud, a coin, a padlock, a light bulb, a clock, an envelope, a delivery truck and a flag join the page, phone and person, and every object takes every look. Vertical cuts keep a world's words whole. When a landscape world is re-framed for a vertical cut, the camera now frames what the authored shot shows rather than everything the beat draws, so a chart's takeaway figure no longer falls off the side. Words the taller frame reaches, including labels carried from earlier beats, are taken in whole while they stay readable, or the frame slides past them. Across the eleven playbooks whose vertical cuts re-frame a world camera, words cut by the frame edge went from 14 to 2. Wide side-by-side compositions still need viewTall, or a portrait layout of their own: a playbook beat's tall props replace its props in a vertical scaffold. concept-explainer's chart now moves its row labels above the bars in portrait. A code change is one scene. A stage that holds only code (an inline before/after, or a commit and file from git) now makes the editor the picture: wide, larger type, centred, with the changed lines lighting up on a spoken word. On a vertical frame long lines wrap at a size a phone reads (34 px) instead of shrinking. How to ask: “Show the before and after of the parser fix as code, then the test that proves it.”

Composed cast objects. A cast object can be a small drawn thing (shape: a page, a speech bubble, a phone, a chart card, a person, a ticket, a parcel) instead of an icon on a tile, and it takes the film's look: hand-drawn and hatched, printed, or flat. How to ask: “Make the reports pages and the customers people.” Casts.

Casts travel. travel sends an object along a curved route to another one, through waypoints if you give them, while a pen trail sketches the way ahead. Draw the world as scenery (stations, a road, a pipe) and let the cast move through it. How to ask: “Draw the support desk, engineering and the customer as three stations, and send one ticket through them.” One ticket, one journey.

Merge and split. A cast can show cause in its shapes: merge folds copies into one object (each flies in and is absorbed while the one it joins pulses), and split bursts several back out of one into a ring around it. How to ask: “Fold the duplicate reports into the first one, then have the fix reach everyone who reported it.” A film with a stage can now start over from a playbook; the stage goes with the scenes it was drawn for. Casts.

Casts hand off to any scene. fill grows one cast object into the whole frame, and the next scene (a chart, a figure, a card, a stage) plays on its colour, with the tone and cuts set for you. emerge brings it back into the cast afterwards. A camera move pushes in on the cast or pulls back, and carries across cuts. How to ask: “Let the chart tile fill the screen and become the real chart, then shrink back into the line.” Casts.

Drawn casts and pen marks. A cast can be drawn by hand (look: "drawn": an ink outline, hatched colour, handwriting) or printed ("print": a dot screen off register). mark moves draw on it as the narration speaks: a loop around what matters, a line under it, a cross through what is set aside, or arrows to others. on gathers objects around one, and every object stays inside the title-safe area. How to ask: “Draw it by hand: cross out the duplicate reports, underline the one that goes first, and draw the replies back to everyone who reported it.”

Safe vertical moves and a cast playbook. On vertical films the slow camera push no longer carries edge type outside the title-safe area: block content starts at 11.5% of the width, and pushes and pans are capped to stay inside 10%. new DIR --playbook process-cast starts a film that follows a few things through a process (requests arrive, repeats leave, one goes first, someone takes it on and it changes). Cast threads now draw only after the pieces they join have arrived, and are left out with a warning if a cue comes too late in the beat.

Casts: objects that carry the story. props.cast keeps a few designed objects (icon tiles, word pills) on screen through consecutive canvas beats. On spoken words they scatter, line up with a thread, let one step forward (hero), turn one into another in place (swap, where by names the cause: a pencil walks onto a chart, scribbles, and the chart changes), pulse in order (wave) and leave. Each beat starts where the last one left every object (place, scale, opacity, depth and threads), so the cuts are invisible. One storyboard serves landscape and vertical. The studio agent's authoring guide now starts from what the audience should see for the material, and matches a mechanism to it (a cast, a stage, a world, morphing charts, or a card) rather than defaulting to cards. How to ask: “Keep the five sources on screen the whole time: pile them up, line them into the story, bring the chart forward, then show the reviewer's note changing it.” Casts · cast study.

Stages and code that fit the frame. A stage code editor now stays inside the picture: its type fits the longest line (measured in the mono face, never below 24 px) and longer lines wrap with a hanging indent instead of being clipped. A stage drawn for a wide frame and shown in a vertical one is laid down the frame in the same order, with a check note, and callouts are kept inside it. Block content on vertical frames now sits inside the middle 80% that the frame audit asks for. Scene engine.

Metric bridges. props.bridge draws a waterfall: a starting total, signed drivers and subtotals that float from the running total, and the new total, in the same frame and type as the other charts. It refuses a bridge that does not add up (and names the missing amount), keeps the totals on an honest zero, switches long names to rows instead of shrinking them, reads down the page on tall frames, and lands drivers on spoken words in reading order. Templates: revenue-bridge and profit-walk in the finance chart kit. Metric bridges.

A simpler studio: notes and an agent. The studio page is rebuilt around what a non-editor does: watch, click the picture to leave a sticky note on that spot and moment, send the notes to the agent, check what it changed, approve. The panels, inspector, timeline lanes, layouts, command palette and Sound tab are gone; the agent does the editing through the same engine-checked, undoable commands, and answers each note it acted on in one line (clearframe_notes answer). Notes keep their spot and the words under it, and the agent reads both. See the studio page.

Build by talking, in the browser. viewer --serve opens on a home page that turns an idea (and any source documents, pictures, footage or sound) into a project with its own OpenCode conversation; the Agent tab carries that conversation beside the picture. A message about a note is held to that note's scene, and the studio refuses agent edits outside it. The agent edits through the same undoable, engine-checked commands as the panels and renders native previews in the same queue. It runs on an isolated OpenCode 2.0.24 with the free Big Pickle model by default. See agent-studio.md.

The scene engine. Every film now renders with ClearFrame's own GPU scene engine (Skia on Metal): a compiled scene plan evaluated frame by frame, with the backdrop, texture, lens, chrome and dissolves composited natively and every block drawn by the engine itself. Native stages add actors that keep their identity, links, packets, callouts, code edits read from a commit, footage as GPU textures, SkSL materials, particles, a 2.5D camera and real motion blur. All 45 playbooks render through it. FFFrames is retired: no FFFrames crate is a dependency, there is no --engine option, and footage is decoded through the ffmpeg/ffprobe tools (bounded, exact final samples, any seek order). Measured speed and memory, per project, are in the coverage report. Scene engine.

A viewer for everyone else. viewer writes one double-clickable HTML page: every film with its versions, a lens over the picture showing each text element's font, size and colour, a scrub timeline with scene, narration, music, sound and note lanes, previews of every project file (images, SVG, video, audio, fonts, 3D), notes pinned to the picture (saved into the film with --serve), and the building blocks in plain language. Version numbers are the revisions' own (r005 is Version 5) and never shift; a note on a version whose video is no longer kept stays listed, labelled with its revision. See viewer.

Footage-led films. Clip assets can select Runway models (seedance2_5 with first and last keyframes, gen4.5 for cheap drafts) alongside Omni and Veo; plan estimates credits and moderation failures explain how to reframe or reroute. Footage-led films covers keyframe stills before motion, last-frame continuation, provider routing and cutting to the measured drop.

Editorial plots and a finance palette. props.plot now fills the frame with a one-line title, a quiet key and axes, values at the line ends, optional regime bands and an area fill; overlapping tick labels fail at authoring time. props.bars adds signed editorial bar charts (vertical or horizontal, honest zero baseline, an optional average line) in the same system; see bars. props.stat is a headline figure that counts up, with a change line coloured by whether a rise is good news (headline figures). props.distribution counts outcomes into bins with a stated threshold share and a latest-year marker (distribution charts). props.multiples puts up to nine small lines on one shared scale with one highlighted (small multiples). The ledger palette (navy, teal, brass) suits financial-services content. Showcase kits and retained render evidence were removed: the library keeps sources, and renders belong in build/.

Footage without a linked decoder. Plates, the video block and stage footage decode through one ffmpeg pipe per open clip, at the size drawn. Timing comes from the clip's own timestamps, the end is exclusive and rounds up to the film's frame grid (a valid final sample stays visible), and backward seeks reproduce frames. The regression tests moved with it. See footage.

Faster review and finishing. QA builds its timeline and phone sheets from one video decode. Final rendering validates the complete muxed file before publishing it. Local same-input checks measured 20% faster QA and 5% faster complete pipeline runs, with identical reports, sheets and MP4 bytes and bounded workers. Blender experiments showed insufficient benefit and were left out. See measurements and limits.

Prepared clips can use a different frame rate from the film. The native decoder honors the final frame’s declared presentation interval and delayed H.264 frames, while a clip that ends before its required picture interval still fails validation.

Question and answer films. Canvas props.teaching supports multiple choice and fill-in-the-blank with separate reading, answer and explanation phases. Optional quiz tiles and closing-gap clips reveal once; retain the unrevealed poster during the question. Timing, answer integrity and 3D integration.

Editable dimensional KPIs. Canvas props.kpi adds headline pedestals, shared-zero comparisons, proportional progress rails, qualitative seesaws and contribution stacks using native shapes and text. Keeping this guide current

This page is meant to change with the project. When a feature lands:

  1. Read the newest CHANGELOG.md section and update What's new. Once a version is released, fold its bullets into one line and start a new list.
  2. Re-run the cheap listing commands (help, treatments, playbooks, themes --json, blocks, motions, sketch) and reconcile the vocabulary tables and counts in the header. New palettes, treatments, sketches and playbooks are files in library/, so git log -- library shows what arrived.
  3. Check new props against schema/storyboard.schema.json and film/canvas.mjs before listing them. Don't document anything you haven't verified.
  4. If a new workflow changes how films are made, add a prompt template or an iteration prompt as well.
  5. Update the Last updated line in the header and the date in the comment at the top of the file.

Structure: one <section id> per topic, in TOC order, with stable ids. Prompt blocks are pre.prompt inside figure.prompt-card, and copy buttons attach automatically. The file is self-contained apart from Google Fonts, and it falls back to system fonts offline.
